Five estimation-theory problems (Kay, Ch. 3): (1) CRLB for the amplitude $A$ of an exponentially/power-weighted DC level $x[n]=Ar^n+w[n]$, and the limiting behavior of the efficient estimator's variance as $N\to\infty$ for $0<r<1$, $r=1$, and $r>1$; (2) CRLB for the base $r$ itself in $x[n]=r^n+w[n]$, showing no unbiased estimator attains it for $N\ge3$ while $N=2$ is efficient and $N=1$ is unidentifiable; (3) CRLB for a DC level in two correlated Gaussian samples, and the limits as $\rho\to\pm1$; (4) the Fisher information matrix for polynomial curve fitting via a Vandermonde design matrix; (5) the limiting variance of the biased estimator $\widehat{\sigma_A^2}=\hat A^2$ in a random-amplitude model, compared against the CRLB.

Problem 1 (3.3)

The data $x[n]=Ar^n+w[n]$ for $n=0,1,\ldots,N-1$ are observed, where $w[n]$ is WGN with variance $\sigma^2$ and $r>0$ is known. Find the CRLB for $A$. Show that an efficient estimator exists and find its variance. What happens to the variance as $N\to\infty$ for various values of $r$?

SOLUTION

The observation model is

$$x[n]=Ar^n+w[n], \qquad n=0,1,\ldots,N-1,$$

where $r>0$ is known and $A$ is the unknown parameter. Here,

$$s[n;A]=Ar^n, \qquad \frac{\partial s[n;A]}{\partial A}=r^n.$$

Substituting into the general WGN formula yields

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat A)\ge \operatorname{CRLB}(A)=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}r^{2n}}.$$

Using the geometric-series formula,

$$\operatorname{CRLB}(A)= \begin{cases} \sigma^2\,\dfrac{1-r^2}{1-r^{2N}}, & r\ne1,\\[0.6em] \dfrac{\sigma^2}{N}, & r=1. \end{cases}$$

To show that an efficient estimator exists, consider

$$\hat A=\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}r^n\,x[n]}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}r^{2n}}.$$

Substituting $x[n]=Ar^n+w[n]$ gives

$$\hat A = A + \frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}r^n\,w[n]}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}r^{2n}}.$$

Since $E[w[n]]=0$, $E[\hat A]=A$, so the estimator is unbiased. Using the independence of the noise samples and $\operatorname{Var}(cX)=c^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$,

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat A) = \frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}r^{2n}\operatorname{Var}(w[n])}{\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}r^{2n}\right)^2} = \frac{\sigma^2\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}r^{2n}}{\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}r^{2n}\right)^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}r^{2n}}.$$
Efficiency $\hat A$ is unbiased and its variance equals the CRLB — it is an efficient estimator.

As $N\to\infty$, the variance behaves as follows:

  • If $0<r<1$: $\operatorname{Var}(\hat A)\to \sigma^2(1-r^2)>0$. The signal decays with $n$, so the total Fisher information remains finite and the estimation variance does not approach zero.
  • If $r=1$: $\operatorname{Var}(\hat A)=\sigma^2/N\to0$, decreasing at the rate $1/N$.
  • If $r>1$: $\operatorname{Var}(\hat A)=\dfrac{\sigma^2(r^2-1)}{r^{2N}-1}\to0$ exponentially, because later observations contain increasingly strong signal.

Problem 2 (3.4)

If $x[n]=r^n+w[n]$ for $n=0,1,\ldots,N-1$ are observed, where $w[n]$ is WGN with variance $\sigma^2$ and $r$ is to be estimated, find the CRLB. Does an efficient estimator exist, and if so, find its variance?

SOLUTION

The observation model is $x[n]=r^n+w[n]$, $n=0,1,\ldots,N-1$, where $r$ is the unknown parameter. Here, $s[n;r]=r^n$. For $n=0$ the signal equals $1$ and its derivative with respect to $r$ is zero; for $n\ge1$,

$$\frac{\partial s[n;r]}{\partial r}=nr^{n-1}.$$

Therefore, for $N\ge2$,

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat r)\ge \operatorname{CRLB}(r)=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}n^2r^{2n-2}}.$$

To determine whether an efficient estimator exists, use the equality condition for the CRLB, $\partial\ell(r)/\partial r = I(r)(\hat r-r)$, where $\ell(r)$ is the log-likelihood and $I(r)$ the Fisher information. For this model,

$$\frac{\partial\ell(r)}{\partial r}=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}nr^{n-1}\bigl(x[n]-r^n\bigr).$$

Let

$$S(r)=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}n^2r^{2n-2}, \qquad I(r)=\frac{S(r)}{\sigma^2}.$$

The equality condition then requires

$$\hat r = r + \frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}nr^{n-1}\bigl(x[n]-r^n\bigr)}{S(r)}.$$

For $N\ge3$, the coefficients of $x[1]$ and $x[2]$ in this expression are

$$a_1(r)=\frac{1}{S(r)}, \qquad a_2(r)=\frac{2r}{S(r)}.$$

Their ratio is $a_2(r)/a_1(r)=2r$, which depends on the unknown parameter. Consequently, no single estimator that depends only on the observations and known quantities can satisfy the equality condition for every $r$.

Result For $N\ge3$, no unbiased estimator attains the CRLB throughout the parameter range.

There are two special sample-size cases:

  • If $N=2$: since $x[1]=r+w[1]$, the estimator $\hat r=x[1]$ satisfies $E[\hat r]=r$, $\operatorname{Var}(\hat r)=\sigma^2=\operatorname{CRLB}(r)$. Hence an efficient estimator exists, with variance $\sigma^2$.
  • If $N=1$: the only observation is $x[0]=1+w[0]$, whose distribution does not depend on $r$. The Fisher information is zero and $r$ is not identifiable.

Problem 3 (3.9)

We observe two samples of a DC level in correlated Gaussian noise,

$$x[0]=A+w[0], \qquad x[1]=A+w[1],$$

where $\mathbf{w}=[w[0]\ w[1]]^T$ is zero mean with covariance matrix

$$\mathbf{C}=\sigma^2\begin{bmatrix}1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1\end{bmatrix}.$$

The parameter $\rho$ is the correlation coefficient between $w[0]$ and $w[1]$. Compute the CRLB for $A$ and compare it to the case when $w[n]$ is WGN ($\rho=0$). Also explain what happens when $\rho\to\pm1$. Finally, comment on the additivity property of the Fisher information for nonindependent observations.

SOLUTION

The two observations can be written as $\mathbf{x}=A\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{w}$, $\mathbf{1}=[1\ 1]^T$, where $\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{C})$, $\mathbf{C}=\sigma^2\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{bmatrix}$. Thus $\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu(A),\mathbf{C})$, $\boldsymbol\mu(A)=A\mathbf{1}$.

First, assume $-1<\rho<1$, so that $\mathbf{C}$ is invertible. Using the general Gaussian Fisher information formula,

$$I(A)=\left[\frac{\partial\boldsymbol\mu(A)}{\partial A}\right]^T\mathbf{C}^{-1} \left[\frac{\partial\boldsymbol\mu(A)}{\partial A}\right] + \frac12\operatorname{tr}\!\left[\left(\mathbf{C}^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{C}}{\partial A}\right)^2\right].$$

Since $\partial\boldsymbol\mu(A)/\partial A=\mathbf{1}$ and $\partial\mathbf{C}/\partial A=0$, the covariance-dependent term vanishes and $I(A)=\mathbf{1}^T\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{1}$. The inverse covariance matrix is

$$\mathbf{C}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\sigma^2(1-\rho^2)}\begin{bmatrix}1&-\rho\\-\rho&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

Consequently,

$$I(A)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2(1-\rho^2)}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}1&-\rho\\-\rho&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix} = \frac{2(1-\rho)}{\sigma^2(1-\rho^2)} = \frac{2}{\sigma^2(1+\rho)}.$$
CRLB $$\operatorname{Var}(\hat A)\ge \operatorname{CRLB}(A)=\frac{\sigma^2(1+\rho)}{2}.$$

This bound is attained by the sample mean $\hat A=(x[0]+x[1])/2$. Indeed, $E[\hat A]=\bigl(E[x[0]]+E[x[1]]\bigr)/2=A$, so the estimator is unbiased. Its variance is

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat A)=\frac14\operatorname{Var}(w[0]+w[1]) = \frac14\Bigl[\operatorname{Var}(w[0])+\operatorname{Var}(w[1])+2\operatorname{Cov}(w[0],w[1])\Bigr] = \frac14(\sigma^2+\sigma^2+2\rho\sigma^2) = \frac{\sigma^2(1+\rho)}{2}.$$

Therefore $\hat A$ is an efficient estimator. For white Gaussian noise, $\rho=0$, giving $\operatorname{CRLB}(A)=\sigma^2/2$.

Positive correlation increases the bound relative to the independent-noise case, whereas negative correlation decreases it. Positively correlated noise tends to reinforce itself when averaged; negatively correlated noise tends to cancel.

As $\rho\to1$, $\operatorname{CRLB}(A)\to\sigma^2$. At $\rho=1$, the noise samples satisfy $w[1]=w[0]$ almost surely, so $x[0]=x[1]=A+w[0]$: the second observation provides no additional information, and averaging does not reduce the noise variance.

As $\rho\to-1$, $\operatorname{CRLB}(A)\to0$. At $\rho=-1$, the noise samples satisfy $w[1]=-w[0]$ almost surely. Therefore,

$$\hat A=\frac{x[0]+x[1]}{2}=A+\frac{w[0]+w[1]}{2}=A.$$
Additivity: for independent observations, Fisher information adds sample by sample ($I_N=N\cdot I_1$). Here the two samples are not independent, so this result shows that correlation modulates — rather than simply accumulates — Fisher information: correlated noise can either inflate the bound (positive $\rho$) or let it collapse to zero (perfect negative correlation, where the noise itself is fully informative about how to cancel it).

Problem 4 (3.13)

Consider a generalization of the line fitting problem described in Example 3.7, termed polynomial or curve fitting. The data model is

$$x[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}A_kn^k+w[n], \qquad n=0,1,\ldots,N-1,$$

where $w[n]$ is WGN with variance $\sigma^2$. It is desired to estimate $\{A_0,A_1,\ldots,A_{p-1}\}$. Find the Fisher information matrix for this problem.

SOLUTION

The unknown parameter vector is $\mathbf{A}=[A_0\ A_1\ \cdots\ A_{p-1}]^T$, and

$$w[n]\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2).$$

The joint likelihood, since the observations are independent, is

$$p(\mathbf{x};\mathbf{A})=(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-N/2} \exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\Bigl(x[n]-\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}A_kn^k\Bigr)^2\right],$$

so the log-likelihood is

$$\ell(\mathbf{A})=-\frac{N}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\Bigl(x[n]-\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}A_kn^k\Bigr)^2.$$

For $i=0,1,\ldots,p-1$, $\partial\bigl(\sum_kA_kn^k\bigr)/\partial A_i=n^i$. Thus

$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial A_i}=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}n^i \Bigl(x[n]-\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}A_kn^k\Bigr), \qquad \frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial A_i\partial A_j}=-\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}n^{i+j}.$$

The $(i,j)$ entry of the Fisher information matrix is $\mathcal{I}_{ij}(\mathbf{A})=-E\bigl[\partial^2\ell/\partial A_i\partial A_j\bigr]$. Since the second derivative does not depend on the observations,

$$\mathcal{I}_{ij}(\mathbf{A})=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}n^{i+j}, \qquad i,j=0,1,\ldots,p-1.$$

Define $S_m=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}n^m$, $S_0=N$ (with $n^0$ taken to be $1$, including at $n=0$). The Fisher information matrix is therefore

$$\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{A})=\frac{1}{\sigma^2} \begin{bmatrix} S_0 & S_1 & \cdots & S_{p-1}\\ S_1 & S_2 & \cdots & S_p\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ S_{p-1} & S_p & \cdots & S_{2p-2} \end{bmatrix}.$$

Equivalently, defining the Vandermonde design matrix

$$\mathbf{H}=\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 1 & 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1\\ 1 & 2 & 2^2 & \cdots & 2^{p-1}\\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 1 & N-1 & (N-1)^2 & \cdots & (N-1)^{p-1} \end{bmatrix},$$
Fisher information matrix $$\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{A})=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\mathbf{H}^T\mathbf{H}.$$

For example, when $p=2$ the model reduces to straight-line fitting, $x[n]=A_0+A_1n+w[n]$, and

$$\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{A})=\frac{1}{\sigma^2} \begin{bmatrix} N & \dfrac{N(N-1)}{2}\\[0.6em] \dfrac{N(N-1)}{2} & \dfrac{N(N-1)(2N-1)}{6} \end{bmatrix}.$$
Because the sampling locations $0,1,\ldots,N-1$ are distinct, $\mathbf{H}$ has full column rank when $N\ge p$, and the Fisher information matrix is invertible. When $N<p$, the matrix is singular and the full coefficient vector is not identifiable.

Problem 5 (3.14)

For the data model in Example 3.11, consider the estimator $\widehat{\sigma_A^2}=(\hat A)^2$, where $\hat A$ is the sample mean. Assume we observe a given data set in which the realization of the random variable $A$ is the value $A_0$. Show that $\hat A\to A_0$ as $N\to\infty$ by verifying that

$$E(\hat A\mid A=A_0)=A_0, \qquad \operatorname{var}(\hat A\mid A=A_0)=\frac{\sigma^2}{N}.$$

Hence $\widehat{\sigma_A^2}\to A_0^2$ as $N\to\infty$ for the given realization $A=A_0$. Next, find the variance of $\widehat{\sigma_A^2}$ as $N\to\infty$ by determining $\operatorname{var}(A^2)$, where $A\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_A^2)$, and compare it to the CRLB. Explain why $\sigma_A^2$ cannot be estimated without error even for $N\to\infty$.

SOLUTION

Consider the random-amplitude model

$$x[n]=A+w[n], \qquad n=0,1,\ldots,N-1, \qquad A\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_A^2), \quad w[n]\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2),$$

where $A$ is independent of the noise, $\sigma^2$ is known, and all $N$ observations share the same realization of $A$. The sample mean is

$$\hat A=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n], \qquad \widehat{\sigma_A^2}=(\hat A)^2.$$

Conditional on the realization $A=A_0$, $x[n]=A_0+w[n]$, so

$$\hat A=A_0+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w[n].$$

Since the noise is independent of $A$ and has zero mean,

$$E[\hat A\mid A=A_0]=A_0+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}E[w[n]\mid A=A_0]=A_0.$$

Furthermore, independence of the noise samples gives

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat A\mid A=A_0) = \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w[n]\right) = \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\operatorname{Var}(w[n]) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N}.$$

Consequently,

$$E\bigl[(\hat A-A_0)^2\mid A=A_0\bigr]=\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\longrightarrow0.$$
Convergence Conditional on $A=A_0$, $\hat A$ converges to $A_0$ in mean square and hence in probability, $\hat A\xrightarrow{P}A_0$. By continuity of the squaring function, $\widehat{\sigma_A^2}=(\hat A)^2\xrightarrow{P}A_0^2$ — the limit is the squared amplitude of the particular realization, not necessarily the population variance $\sigma_A^2$.

To determine the variation across realizations, note that a zero-mean Gaussian random variable satisfies $E[A^2]=\sigma_A^2$, $E[A^4]=3\sigma_A^4$. Therefore,

$$\operatorname{Var}(A^2)=E[A^4]-\bigl(E[A^2]\bigr)^2=3\sigma_A^4-\sigma_A^4=2\sigma_A^4.$$

The finite-sample variance confirms this limit. Since $\hat A=A+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w[n]$ is the sum of independent Gaussian random variables,

$$\hat A\sim\mathcal N\!\left(0,\ \sigma_A^2+\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\right).$$

Hence,

$$\operatorname{Var}\bigl(\widehat{\sigma_A^2}\bigr)=2\left(\sigma_A^2+\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\right)^2, \qquad \lim_{N\to\infty}\operatorname{Var}\bigl(\widehat{\sigma_A^2}\bigr)=2\sigma_A^4.$$

To compare this result with the CRLB, let $\tau=\sigma_A^2>0$. After marginalizing over $A$, the observation vector has distribution $\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf{C}(\tau))$, where

$$\mathbf{C}(\tau)=\tau\,\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T+\sigma^2\mathbf{I}_N,$$

with $\mathbf{1}$ the $N$-dimensional vector of ones — the observations are correlated because they share the same random amplitude. Since the mean does not depend on $\tau$, the Gaussian Fisher information formula reduces to

$$I(\tau)=\frac12\operatorname{tr}\!\left[\left(\mathbf{C}^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{C}}{\partial\tau}\right)^2\right].$$

We have $\partial\mathbf{C}/\partial\tau=\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T$, and $\mathbf{C}\mathbf{1}=(\sigma^2+N\tau)\mathbf{1}$, which implies $\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}/(\sigma^2+N\tau)$. Therefore,

$$I(\tau)=\frac12\bigl(\mathbf{1}^T\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{1}\bigr)^2 = \frac12\left(\frac{N}{\sigma^2+N\tau}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{2(\tau+\sigma^2/N)^2}.$$
CRLB vs. limiting variance $$\operatorname{CRLB}(\sigma_A^2)=2\left(\sigma_A^2+\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\right)^2 \ \xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\ 2\sigma_A^4.$$ The limiting variance of the proposed estimator equals the limiting CRLB.

For finite $N$, however, $E\bigl[\widehat{\sigma_A^2}\bigr]=\sigma_A^2+\sigma^2/N$, so the estimator is biased. An unbiased version is

$$\widetilde{\sigma_A^2}=(\hat A)^2-\frac{\sigma^2}{N},$$

which has the same variance and attains the finite-sample CRLB.

Why $\sigma_A^2$ can never be estimated exactly: even as $N\to\infty$, all $N$ observations share the same single realization of $A$ — extra samples only average out the observation noise $w[n]$ and pin down that one realization $A_0$ exactly, never additional independent draws of $A$ itself. Estimating $\sigma_A^2$ from one draw of a Gaussian is like estimating a variance from a sample size of one: the CRLB floor $2\sigma_A^4$ is the irreducible cost of only ever seeing $A$ once.
