ECG 762 HW4 — MVU Estimation in Linear Models: Exponential Amplitudes, Fourier Signals, Whitening, and Random Design

Four problems from Chapter 4 connect Gaussian linear estimation to signal reconstruction, whitening, and random design matrices. The derivations distinguish parameter estimates from estimated signals, and weights on whitened observations from weights on raw observations.

Problem 4.1 — Amplitudes of exponentials

For known real $r_i$, observe $x[n]=\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_i r_i^n+w[n]$, $n=0,\ldots,N-1$, with white Gaussian noise of variance $\sigma^2$. Define $H_{ni}=r_i^n$ and write $\mathbf x=\mathbf H\mathbf A+\mathbf w$. If $\mathbf H$ has full column rank, the Gaussian linear-model estimator is

$$\widehat{\mathbf A}=(\mathbf H^T\mathbf H)^{-1}\mathbf H^T\mathbf x.$$

Substitution gives $\widehat{\mathbf A}=\mathbf A+(\mathbf H^T\mathbf H)^{-1}\mathbf H^T\mathbf w$, so

$$E[\widehat{\mathbf A}]=\mathbf A,\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\widehat{\mathbf A})=\sigma^2(\mathbf H^T\mathbf H)^{-1}.$$

The estimator attains the CRLB and is MVU. Distinct real $r_i$ and $N\ge p$ ensure the Vandermonde design has full column rank. For computation,

$$[\mathbf H^T\mathbf H]_{ij}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(r_i r_j)^n,\qquad [\mathbf H^T\mathbf x]_i=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}r_i^n x[n].$$

Constant and alternating components

For $p=2$, $r_1=1$, $r_2=-1$, and even $N$, the two columns are orthogonal because $\sum_n(-1)^n=0$. Thus $\mathbf H^T\mathbf H=N\mathbf I_2$ and

$$\widehat A_1=\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n],\qquad \widehat A_2=\frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(-1)^n x[n].$$
ResultBoth estimates have variance $\sigma^2/N$ and zero covariance. Since they are jointly Gaussian, they are independent. Ordinary averaging isolates the constant component; alternating-sign averaging isolates the other component.

Problem 4.4 — Distribution of the estimated signal

The true signal $\mathbf s=\mathbf H\boldsymbol\theta$ is deterministic. Its estimate $\widehat{\mathbf s}=\mathbf H\widehat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ is a linear transformation of a Gaussian parameter estimate, so

$$\widehat{\mathbf s}\sim\mathcal N\!\left(\mathbf s,\sigma^2\mathbf H(\mathbf H^T\mathbf H)^{-1}\mathbf H^T\right).$$

When $p<N$, this covariance has rank $p$: the distribution is supported on the column space of $\mathbf H$ and has no ordinary $N$-dimensional density.

Fourier analysis in Example 4.2

$$s[n]=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left[a_k\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi kn}{N}\right)+b_k\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi kn}{N}\right)\right],\qquad M<\frac N2.$$

The sine and cosine columns are orthogonal, giving $\mathbf H^T\mathbf H=(N/2)\mathbf I_{2M}$. Consequently,

$$\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat s[n],\widehat s[m])=\frac{2\sigma^2}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi k(n-m)}{N}\right).$$ $$\operatorname{Var}(\widehat s[n])=\frac{2M\sigma^2}{N},\qquad \widehat s[n]\sim\mathcal N\!\left(s[n],\frac{2M\sigma^2}{N}\right).$$

For fixed $M$, increasing the number of observations reduces each estimated sample's variance.

Problem 4.10 — Unequal noise variances and whitening

For $x[n]=A+w[n]$ with $\mathbf C=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_0^2,\ldots,\sigma_{N-1}^2)$, initially assume every variance is positive. Whiten using $\mathbf D=\operatorname{diag}(1/\sigma_0,\ldots,1/\sigma_{N-1})$, so $x'[n]=x[n]/\sigma_n$.

The textbook's coefficients $d_n$ multiply the whitened data:

$$d_n=\frac{1/\sigma_n}{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}1/\sigma_k^2},\qquad \widehat A=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}d_n x'[n].$$

On the original observations, the weights are instead inverse-variance weights:

$$\alpha_n=\frac{1/\sigma_n^2}{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}1/\sigma_k^2},\qquad \widehat A=\frac{\sum_n x[n]/\sigma_n^2}{\sum_n1/\sigma_n^2}.$$ $$\sum_n\alpha_n=1,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\widehat A)=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac1{\sigma_n^2}\right)^{-1}.$$

More reliable observations receive greater weight; equal variances recover the sample mean. If $\sigma_j^2=0$, then $w[j]=0$ almost surely and $x[j]=A$. Use $\widehat A=x[j]$, with zero variance. The covariance matrix is then singular, so directly substituting into an inverse-covariance formula is invalid.

Problem 4.13 — A random design matrix

Suppose the realized $\mathbf H$ is observed, has full column rank almost surely, and the required moments exist. Set $\mathbf B(\mathbf H)=(\mathbf H^T\mathbf H)^{-1}\mathbf H^T$. Then $\widehat{\boldsymbol\theta}=\boldsymbol\theta+\mathbf B(\mathbf H)\mathbf w$.

If $\mathbf H$ and $\mathbf w$ are independent, conditioning on $\mathbf H$ gives

$$E[\widehat{\boldsymbol\theta}\mid\mathbf H]=\boldsymbol\theta,\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\widehat{\boldsymbol\theta}\mid\mathbf H)=\sigma^2(\mathbf H^T\mathbf H)^{-1}.$$

Total expectation and total covariance therefore yield

$$E[\widehat{\boldsymbol\theta}]=\boldsymbol\theta,\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\widehat{\boldsymbol\theta})=\sigma^2 E_{\mathbf H}\!\left[(\mathbf H^T\mathbf H)^{-1}\right].$$

The inverse is taken before the expectation. Without independence, the conditional noise mean can be nonzero:

$$E[\widehat{\boldsymbol\theta}]=\boldsymbol\theta+E_{\mathbf H}\!\left[\mathbf B(\mathbf H)E[\mathbf w\mid\mathbf H]\right].$$

For a scalar counterexample, take $w\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ and $H=\operatorname{sign}(w)$. Then $\widehat\theta=\theta+|w|$, so

$$E[\widehat\theta]=\theta+\sigma\sqrt{\frac2\pi},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\widehat\theta)=\sigma^2\left(1-\frac2\pi\right).$$
Independence is sufficient but not necessary. The original conclusions still hold if $E[\mathbf w\mid\mathbf H]=\mathbf0$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf w\mid\mathbf H)=\sigma^2\mathbf I$.
